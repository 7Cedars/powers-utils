# NIST 2024 — AI RMF: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile

**Citation:** National Institute of Standards and Technology. 2024. *Artificial Intelligence Risk
Management Framework: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile.* NIST AI 600-1.
https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.AI.600-1.
**Analytical level:** Structural + Dynamic
**Most relevant design decisions:** Q1, Q3, Q7

This source is the generative-AI-specific companion to `nist_ai_rmf_2023.md`; it should be read
alongside that guide rather than duplicating its GOVERN-function structural material. The content
extracted here is specific to risks that are unique to, or substantially worse for, generative
(LLM-based) AI systems specifically — directly relevant wherever an AI agent (rather than a
narrow, deterministic model) is the one deciding how to call a Powers mandate.

---

## Q1 — Role Structure

§2.7 (Human-AI Configuration): "due to the complexity and increasing reliability of GAI
technology... humans may over-rely on GAI systems or may unjustifiably perceive GAI content to be
of higher quality than that produced by other sources. This phenomenon is... automation bias."
This directly qualifies the human-oversight-role recommendation already drawn from the companion
RMF guide (`nist_ai_rmf_2023.md`, Q1): creating a separate human-held oversight role is necessary
but not sufficient, because a human overseer paired with a fluent, confident-sounding GAI agent is
specifically prone to rubber-stamping rather than genuinely reviewing. Two concrete mitigations for
Powers mandate design: (1) design the human-oversight mandate to require an affirmative,
substantive action (e.g., a `StatementOfIntent` whose `inputParams` require a stated rationale)
rather than a bare approve/reject or a silence-as-approval `timelock`, since low-friction
rubber-stamp paths are exactly where automation bias shows up; (2) where feasible, keep the
human reviewer's incentives independent from the agent's throughput (e.g., don't route the
reviewer's compensation through the same treasury mandate the agent is trying to trigger), since a
reviewer with a stake in fast approval has a second reason to under-scrutinize.

## Q3 — Mandate Selection

§2.9 (Information Security): "prompt injection involves modifying what input is provided to a GAI
system so that it behaves in unintended ways... Indirect prompt injection attacks occur when
adversaries remotely... exploit LLM-integrated applications by injecting prompts into data likely
to be retrieved," with demonstrated consequences including "stealing proprietary data or running
malicious code remotely." This is the single most concrete and novel risk in this source for
Powers designs that grant an AI agent a role: whenever the agent's decision about *how* to call a
mandate is influenced by external, attacker-reachable content (a proposal description, a linked
document, retrieved data), that content is an attack surface, not just an input. An attacker who
cannot directly hold `allowedRole` for a mandate may still be able to manipulate an agent that
does hold it, by crafting content the agent will process. The mandate-selection implication:
- Prefer `BespokeAction_Simple`/`PresetActions`-style mandates with tightly bound `target` and
  `value` ranges over broad `OpenAction` grants for any role an AI agent holds, so that even a
  successfully manipulated agent is mechanically constrained to a narrow blast radius — the
  mandate's scope, not the agent's judgment, is the actual security boundary.
- Treat any content an agent ingests from outside the constitution's own on-chain state (web
  pages, uploaded documents, other agents' messages) as adversarial input by default, and avoid
  routing it into `inputParams` for high-stakes mandates without an intermediate validation step
  (see `erc_8004_2025.md` Q3, validation-gated trust model) that does not itself rely on the same
  agent's judgment.

## Q7 — Accountability and Monitoring

§2.2/§2.8 (Confabulation / Information Integrity): GAI systems can produce "confidently stated
but erroneous or false content," and because this content can be produced "at scale," it risks
polluting exactly the kind of textual record that on-chain governance depends on for
accountability. This is a Powers-specific risk not covered by the human-authored-accountability
assumptions elsewhere in this library: if an AI-agent role authors the descriptive text in a
`StatementOfIntent` (a stated rationale, a proposal description), that text can be fluent,
specific, and entirely fabricated — and once written on-chain it becomes a permanent part of the
audit trail described in `erc_8004_2025.md` Q7 as immutable ("on-chain pointers and hashes cannot
be deleted"). A confabulated rationale is worse than no rationale, because it looks like evidence.
Mitigation: flag agent-authored text distinctly from human-authored text in any mandate config or
convention used for descriptions (e.g., a reserved `inputParams` field or a required prefix), and
do not treat an agent's stated rationale as verified evidence of anything beyond "the agent
produced this text" — verify any factual claim it makes through an independent channel before
relying on it for a consequential decision.

§2.12 (Value Chain and Component Integration): GAI systems typically depend on "many distinct
third-party components and data sources," to the point that "it may be difficult to attribute
issues in a system's behavior to any one of these sources" when something goes wrong. This
sharpens the third-party-dependency contingency point already made in `nist_ai_rmf_2023.md` (Q3):
for an AI-agent role specifically, post-hoc accountability may be structurally limited, because
even a full on-chain audit trail of the agent's *actions* cannot necessarily explain *why* the
underlying model produced them — the fault may sit in an opaque training pipeline no on-chain
record can reach. Design implication: for agent-held roles, weight ex-ante mandate-scope limits
(narrow `target`/`value` bounds, mandatory `timelock`, validation gates) more heavily relative to
ex-post accountability chains (`RevokeAccountsRoleId` after the fact) than would be appropriate for
a human-held role, since the ex-post chain's diagnostic value is weaker when the failure's root
cause may be unattributable in principle, not just in practice.

## What to skip

Most of §2 (CBRN Information, Dangerous/Violent/Hateful Content, Data Privacy, Environmental
Impacts, Harmful Bias and Homogenization, Intellectual Property, Obscene/Degrading/Abusive
Content) and all of §3 (the detailed suggested-actions checklist) address content-safety,
model-training, and legal/IP risks specific to operating or deploying a generative model as a
product — relevant to whoever configures the underlying AI system, but without a governance-
mandate analogue for the Powers constitution that later grants that system a role. These are
concerns for the "provider" role identified in `fli_ai_act_overview_2024.md` (Q1), not for
constitution design itself.

---

## Mandate implications

- Require human-oversight mandates paired with an AI-agent-held role to demand an affirmative,
  substantive action (a stated rationale) rather than a low-friction approve/reject or
  silence-as-approval path, to counteract automation-bias rubber-stamping.
- Grant AI-agent roles narrowly-scoped mandates (`BespokeAction_Simple`/`PresetActions` with tight
  `target`/`value` bounds) rather than broad `OpenAction` grants, so mandate scope — not agent
  judgment — is the actual security boundary against prompt-injection-driven manipulation.
- Treat any externally-sourced content an agent processes as adversarial input by default; do not
  route it into high-stakes `inputParams` without an independent validation step.
- Flag agent-authored on-chain text (rationale, descriptions) as distinct from human-authored
  text, and do not treat an agent's stated rationale as verified evidence without independent
  confirmation.
- For agent-held roles, weight ex-ante scope limits more heavily relative to ex-post accountability
  chains, since post-hoc fault attribution may be structurally limited by opacity in the agent's
  underlying model/training pipeline.
